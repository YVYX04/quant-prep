2.2.1 Birthday problem

Problem

You and your colleagues know the following information (public knowledge). Your boss A’s birthday is one of the following 10 dates:

March: 4, 5, 8
June: 4, 7
September: 1, 5
December: 1, 2, 8

A told you only the month of his birthday, and told you colleague C only the day. After that you said:

“I don’t know A’s birthday; C doesn’t know either.”

After hearing this, C said:

“I did not know the date, but now I know it.”

You smiled and said:

“Now, I know it too!”.

What is the date of A’s birthday?

Solution

  1. We don’t know the date and knows that C does not either. How is it possible for us to know this? Because we know that the date’s day is necessarily repeated. Hence, the possible candidates month becomes: March and September. Once we communicate this, C can also infer this.
  2. C was directly able to identify A’s birthday date. How? We need to remove all the dates with day 5. Then, C has enough information to find among: Sep. 1, Mar. 4, or Mar. 8. C communicates.
  3. Now, A is able to find it, meaning that it must be Sep. 1.

2.2.2 Card Game

Problem

A casino offers a card game using a normal deck of 52 cards. The rule is that you turn over two cards each time. For each pair, if both are black, they go to the dealer’s pile, if both are red, to yours. Otherwise, they are discarded. The process is repeated until we go through the 52 cards. If you have strictly more cards in your pile, you win \$ 100; otherwise, nothing. Being rational, how much are you willing to pay to play the game?

Solution

Assumption 1: the cards are not drawn from the “dealer’s pile”.

This game is purely deterministic. Indeed, whenever we draw two red cards, we automatically loose one opportunity to counter the black cards in the future (i.e. at least one pair of two black cards will necessarily be drawn from the pile). Therefore, in the end, the two players end up with exactly the same number of cards in their pile. However, since the house wins in the event of a draw, we will always loose. As such, we should not invest any money in this game.

2.2.3 Burning ropes

Problem

You have two different ropes r1 and r2. Each rope will take exactly one hour to burn. However, both ropes have different densities at different points, so there is no guarantee that they will burn regularly. Given that the density patterns are not necessarily the same for each rope, how do you use the two ropes to measure 45 minutes?

Solution

  • For rope r1, we can light both ends of the rope so that it burns twice as fast: 30 minutes.
  • Simultaneously, when we light both ends of r1, we light only one end of rope r2.
  • Exactly when rope r1 has finished burning, we know that 30 minutes have passed. At this point, rope r2 would still take another 30 minutes to burn completely. However, if we light the other end of r2 now, it will take only half that time, so 15 minutes.
  • Once r2 has finished burning, exactly 45 minutes will have passed.

Solution

We put 6 balls on each side of the scale. We spot the heaviest group (the heavy ball is indicate by a 1):

The heaviest ball will be in one of the three groups and the operation allows to determine which one (as illustrated by the schema). Finally, we will only have two balls to measure.

2.2.5 Trailing zeros

Problem

How many trailing zeros are there in 100!?

Solution

A trailing 0 will appear once we multiply by 2 and 5. For instance:

0! = 1, 1! = 1, 2! = 2, 3! = 6, 4! = 24, 5! = 120.

Therefore, we need to count the number of paired (2, 5) until reaching 100. Evidently, the number 2 will appear far more often than the number 5; thus, 5 is an upper bound. We now need to determine how many times the number 5 appears.

First of all, we have 20 multiples of 5 until 100. However, when we multiply by 25, we have two occurences of 5. This increases the count to 21. We also have 50 = 5 \times 5 \times 2, so our count reaches 22. We do not forget 75 = 5 \times 5 \times 3. Then, 100 = 2 \times 5 \times 2 \times 5. So our final count reaches 24.

We will have 24 trailing 0s in 100!.

2.2.7 Infinite Sequence

Problem

If x^x^x^x^x^x^(...) = 2, where x^x = x^x, what is x?

Solution

Let

y = x^{x^{x^{x^{\cdot^{\cdot^\cdot}}}}}.

We are given that y=2. Because removing the bottom x from an infinite power sequence leaves the same sequence,

y=x^y.

Substituting y=2,

2=x^2 \implies x = \sqrt{2}
